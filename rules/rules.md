OCHO

Match. Play. Win.

Objective

Be the first player to empty your hand.

OCHO is a fast-paced card game of matching, timing, and calculated disruption. Players take turns adding cards to the center pile by matching the active color, number, or symbol.

But watch your opponents carefully — the right special card at the right moment can completely change the game.

2. Setup

OCHO is designed for 2–6 players.

Shuffle the entire deck.

Deal 7 cards to each player.

Place the remaining cards face-down in the center. This is the Draw Pile.

Turn the top card of the Draw Pile face-up beside it. This begins the Play Pile.

If the first card is a special card, Wild card, or Color Crush card, return it to the Draw Pile, shuffle, and reveal another card.

The player to the dealer's left takes the first turn.

Players keep their cards hidden from the other players.

3. Taking Your Turn

On your turn, you may play one card onto the Play Pile.

Your card must match the top card by at least one of these properties:

Color

Number

Symbol

For example, if the top card is a Red 7, you may play:

Any Red card

Any 7

A Wild card

A Color Crush card

You may not play a Yellow 4, for example, because it shares neither the color nor the number.

After playing a card, your turn ends unless the card specifically creates another action.

4. Drawing a Card

If you cannot or do not want to play a card, draw one card from the Draw Pile.

If the drawn card can legally be played, you may immediately play it.

If you cannot play the drawn card, your turn ends.

Players are never required to reveal why they chose to draw.

An 8 drawn this way follows the normal matching rules. The OCHO Escape does not activate during a normal one-card draw.

5. The Special Cards

OCHO uses special cards to create opportunities to disrupt the flow of the game.

SKIP

When a Skip card is played, the next player loses their turn.

Play then continues with the following player.

The Skip card's color remains active.

TURN

When a Turn card is played, the direction of play changes.

If play was moving clockwise, it now moves counterclockwise.

If play was moving counterclockwise, it now moves clockwise.

In a two-player game, a Turn card causes the player who played it to take another turn.

The Turn card's color remains active.

DRAW 2

When a Draw 2 card is played, the next player must draw up to two cards, one at a time, and loses their turn.

If the player draws an 8 during the penalty, The OCHO Escape activates and ends the draw early.

The Draw 2 card's color remains active unless The OCHO Escape puts an 8 of another color onto the Play Pile.

Draw 2 cards cannot be chained together.

6. Wild Cards and Color Crush

WILD

A Wild card can be played on any card.

When you play a Wild card, you choose which color becomes active.

Announce the chosen color before play continues.

For example:

"Blue!"

Blue is now the active color.

The next player must play a Blue card, a Wild card, or a Color Crush card.

A Wild card has no color of its own.

COLOR CRUSH

A Color Crush card can be played on any card.

When you play a Color Crush card:

Choose and announce the new active color.

The next player must draw up to four cards, one at a time.

That player's turn ends after the penalty is resolved.

Play continues using the chosen color unless The OCHO Escape changes it.

Color Crush has no color of its own and may be played whether or not you have another playable card.

If the penalized player does not draw an 8, they draw all four cards and do not play a card during that turn.

7. The OCHO Escape

The OCHO Escape may activate only while a player is drawing cards because of a Draw 2 or Color Crush penalty.

Penalty cards are drawn one at a time. If the penalized player draws an 8:

The draw penalty ends immediately.

The player immediately plays the 8 onto the Play Pile, regardless of the active color.

The player keeps any cards drawn before the 8.

The played 8's color becomes the new active color, replacing any color chosen for Color Crush.

The player's penalty turn is over, and play continues with the next player in the current direction.

The 8 is not added to the player's hand before being played. It is placed directly onto the Play Pile as part of resolving the penalty.

Example

A player plays Color Crush and chooses Red. The next player draws, in order:

Yellow 3 → Blue 11 → Green 8

The player stops after the third card, keeps the Yellow 3 and Blue 11, and immediately plays the Green 8. Green becomes the active color, and the fourth card is not drawn.

If the first penalty card drawn is an 8, the player immediately plays it and draws no additional cards.

Important Limitation

An 8 is not normally Wild. An 8 already in a player's hand must be played by matching its color or number, just like any other number card.

Drawing an 8 during the normal one-card draw on your turn also does not activate The OCHO Escape. It may be played immediately only if it matches under the normal rules. The two-card penalty for failing to call OCHO does not activate The OCHO Escape either.

8. The OCHO Moment

Whenever you play a card and have exactly one card remaining, you must immediately say:

"OCHO!"

This announces that you are down to your final card.

If another player catches you before the next player begins their turn, you must draw two cards as a penalty.

Once the next player has begun their turn, it is too late to call you out.

If you forget to say OCHO and another player calls it before the next turn begins, draw two cards.

This requirement also applies when you play an 8 through The OCHO Escape.

9. Going Out

When you play your final card, you win the round.

The round ends immediately.

You do not need to draw back up or continue playing after someone has emptied their hand.

10. Scoring

After a player wins a round, the winner receives points based on the cards remaining in every opponent's hand.

Number Cards

Worth their printed value.

1 = 1 point

2 = 2 points

...

12 = 12 points

Special Cards

Skip = 20 points

Turn = 20 points

Draw 2 = 25 points

Colorless Cards

Wild = 40 points

Color Crush = 50 points

Add the points from all opponents' remaining cards to the winner's score.

11. Winning the Game

Continue playing rounds until one player reaches 300 points.

The player with the highest score wins.

If multiple players reach or exceed 300 points during the same round, the player with the highest total score wins.

12. The Draw Pile Runs Out

If the Draw Pile becomes empty:

Leave the top card of the Play Pile where it is.

Collect the remaining cards in the Play Pile.

Shuffle them thoroughly.

Place them face-down to create a new Draw Pile.

Continue playing.

13. Two-Player Rules

OCHO works particularly well with two players, but a few rules change:

Turn Cards

Playing a Turn card gives you another turn.

Skip Cards

Playing a Skip card also gives you another turn because the other player is skipped.

Draw 2 Cards

The opponent resolves the draw penalty and loses their turn, allowing you to play again. If they trigger The OCHO Escape, they immediately play the 8 and play then returns to you.

Color Crush Cards

The opponent resolves the draw penalty and loses their turn, allowing you to play again. If they trigger The OCHO Escape, they immediately play the 8 and play then returns to you using the 8's color.

14. Strategy

OCHO isn't only about getting rid of cards quickly.

Keep an eye on:

Your opponents' colors

If someone repeatedly draws instead of playing, they may be missing a particular color.

Your final card

Don't wait until the last moment to think about your OCHO call. If your final card is difficult to play, you may want to preserve a matching card earlier in the round.

Special cards

A Skip or Turn card can be more valuable strategically than simply playing a number card.

Eights

An 8 in your hand is an ordinary number card, but an 8 found during a draw penalty becomes an immediate escape. A Color Crush can be devastating, but it always carries the chance that the next player will turn the penalty into a color-changing defense.

Your opponent's hand size

Sometimes forcing an opponent to draw is more valuable than getting rid of your own highest-value card.
